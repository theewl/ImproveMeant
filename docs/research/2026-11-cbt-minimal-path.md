# CBT for social / speaking anxiety — the minimal effective path (2026-11)

## 1. Which components carry the effect?

### 1a. Dropping safety behaviours + turning attention outward (strong, early, cheap)
- **McManus et al. 2009** (*J Anxiety Disord* 23:496–503; doi 10.1016/j.janxdis.2008.10.010). Isolated the "self-focused attention and safety behaviours (SFA/SB) experiment" with video feedback from full CT-SAD in a clinical SAD sample and found significant change in cognition and social anxiety in the week after it; it is usually run in the first phase of treatment. Sample size and effect sizes not visible in snippets. [S] https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/179644/2/Driving%20cognitive%20change%20a%20guide%20to%20behavioural%20experiments%20in%20cognitive%20therapy%20for%20anxiety%20disorders%20and%20PTSD.pdf ; https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publications/176718 — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Wells & Papageorgiou 1998** (*Behavior Therapy* 29:357–370; doi 10.1016/S0005-7894(98)80037-3). Within-subject, counterbalanced: one session of exposure alone vs one of exposure + external attention focus. "Exposure plus external attention focus was significantly more effective than exposure alone in reducing within-situation anxiety and belief in feared catastrophes," and shifted self-imagery from observer to field perspective. Tiny sample. [S] https://www.scilit.net/publications/a008b0f1d2eafcd2f4cc15d1c7d9c1ea — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Taylor, Plasencia & Alden 2011** ("Differential effects of safety behaviour subtypes in SAD") and **Plasencia et al. 2016**: in a conversation task, dropping safety behaviours increased felt authenticity and desire to interact, and an earlier study found partners rated the person better when safety behaviours were dropped. Journal attribution not confirmed in snippets. [S] https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DF904C2C3D6971111045C3C620C2C53B/core-reader — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Furukawa et al. 2009** (role plays with vs without SB/SFA): observable anxiety, belief in feared outcome and overall performance were all better without SB/SFA, but dropping them alone "was unable to completely correct the cognitive distortion," which is the argument for adding feedback. [S] https://webview.isho.jp/journal/detail/abs/10.11477/mf.1405101260 — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Caution:** a 2025 RCT of purely digital safety-behaviour fading (Zech et al.; text-message reminders) found no between-group difference on social anxiety at post or follow-up. Unsupported "just stop your safety habits" nudges may not be enough on their own. [S] https://nationalsocialanxietycenter.com/research-summaries/safety-behavior-fading-social-anxiety/ — accessed 2026-11-02

### 1b. Video / observer feedback (strong for the self-image, needs preparation)
- **Warnock-Parkes et al. 2017**, "Seeing is believing" (*Cogn Behav Pract* 24:245–255; n = 47). "Significant reductions in social anxiety were observed in the week following video feedback and these exceeded those observed in a control week." Processing biases can cancel the effect "if not addressed," so the paper prescribes preparation: predict in detail how you will look, then watch as a stranger would, then compare. [S] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5627505/ ; https://nationalsocialanxietycenter.com/research-summaries/video-feedback-for-social-anxiety/ — accessed 2026-11-02
- Implication: Clearline's observer listen-back only works if the **prediction comes first** and is specific, and if the slider defaults don't pre-fill the answer (see code-review B #1).

### 1c. Exposure vs exposure + cognitive restructuring (restructuring adds little as a separate step; expectancy work matters)
- **Feske & Chambless 1995** meta-analysis (*Behavior Therapy* 26:695–720; 21 studies): CBT (n=12) and exposure-only (n=9) "are equally effective"; CBT "did not lead to greater pretest–posttest or pretest–follow-up improvement" on social anxiety, cognitive or mood measures. [S] https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/990206560 — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Hope, Heimberg & Bruch 1995** dismantling: exposure alone at least as effective as exposure + cognitive intervention. **Hofmann 2004** (*JCCP* 72:588–596; n=90): symptom change tracked change in negative self-focused thoughts only in the CBGT arm, i.e. the cognitive component matters for mechanism even when outcomes tie. [S] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1475946 — accessed 2026-11-02
- **de Jong & Lommen 2021** (65 adolescents with speech anxiety, 5-session school groups): "no meaningful difference" between exposure-only, cognitive-then-exposure, or relaxation-then-exposure; authors conclude CBT for speech anxiety "could be streamlined to exposure." [S] https://www.universiteitleiden.nl/en/research/research-projects/social-and-behavioural-sciences/a-micro-trial-study-to-investigate-the-effectiveness-of-public-speaking-treatments-in-children — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Johnco, Norberg, Wuthrich & Rapee 2025** (*JCCP*; 249 adults with elevated public-speaking anxiety). Three arms: behavioural experiments (predict → do → compare), cognitive restructuring before exposure, exposure without explicit expectancy work. "The BE and CR + EXP groups showed superior anxiety reduction … and threat expectancy change … compared to exposure without explicit processing of threat expectancies." BE had a small, non-significant edge over CR+EXP, larger expectancy change, and **shorter treatment**. Expectancy change during exposure predicted outcome. [S] https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-restructuring-before-exposure-therapy-or-behavioral-exp/ ; https://lighthouse.mq.edu.au/article/may-2025/fear-of-public-speaking-can-be-conquered-new-research — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Reading of the whole set:** a stand-alone "restructure your thought" worksheet is the most dispensable ingredient. What is not dispensable is making the person **name a feared outcome and check it against what happened**. The best place to do the thinking is *inside* the brave step (prediction before, comparison after), not in a separate thought-record module. This is exactly the behavioural-experiment format our app already has.

### 1d. Brief protocols (promising, small samples)
- **One-week iCBT for SAD** (UNSW / Black Dog, *J Anxiety Disord* 2025; n=61, clinician-supported, 6 lessons in 7 days vs waitlist): superior on social anxiety and impairment at post and 1-month follow-up; 84% completed, 85% satisfied, ~11% found the pace "overwhelming or stressful." One-month follow-up only. [S] https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/fast-effective-online-therapy-helps-people-overcome-social-anxiety ; https://newatlas.com/health-wellbeing/online-cbt-social-anxiety-disorder — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Single-session VR exposure for public-speaking anxiety** (Banakou, Slater et al., *JMIR Formative Res* 2024; n=45, non-clinical): single session "at least as effective … as the multiple-session exposure," both beat control; authors call it preliminary. [S] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11301124 — accessed 2026-11-02
- Implication: a short, dense path is defensible. The limiting factor is pacing, not content volume.

**Pattern:** every programme is a **single linear path**: understand the loop → one short thinking skill → the bulk of the time on graded doing (exposure / experiments) → maintenance. Thinking work is always a prelude to, or wrapper around, doing, never a parallel toolbox. The Joyable data is the clearest signal that the *exposure* module is where the effect and the drop-out both live, so the path should get people doing a brave step as early as possible.

## 3. Simplicity principles from digital CBT

- **Adherence is the bottleneck, not content.** App RCTs for depression/anxiety average 17% post-test and 27% follow-up attrition (JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis); open-access programmes do far worse (Joyable 16% full adherence). [S] https://jamanetwork-com.libproxy.ajou.ac.kr/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2841175 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28650193 — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Funnel losses happen at the first screen.** In PTSD Coach only 80% of first-time users reached the home screen and 37% opened a primary content area. [S] https://mental.jmir.org/2015/1/e7/PDF — accessed 2026-11-02
- **"Overwhelming" is a stated reason for leaving.** Thematic analysis of 13,549 reviews across 106 mental-health apps (Alqahtani & Orji 2020, *Health Informatics J*) found usability and content issues drive dissatisfaction; individual reviews of CBT-style apps cite "having to check multiple areas and unfamiliar terminology." [S] https://www.pubmed.gov/31920160/ ; https://appshunter.io/ios/app/1063365447/reviews — accessed 2026-11-02
- **Engagement features correlate with outcome**, but a meta-analysis found an unexpected *negative* association between the number of persuasive-design features and engagement, i.e. more features is not more engagement. [S] https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1038/s41746-021-00386-8.pdf — accessed 2026-11-02
- **What the evidence-backed programmes actually do** (This Way Up, Joyable, the one-week iCBT): numbered lessons in a fixed order, one skill per lesson, a worked example carried by a character's story, homework that is the next lesson's input, and a human-style check-in. No source in this session tested "linear vs toolbox" head-to-head; treat the design pattern as consistent practice, not proven causation. [S] sources in §2
- **Pacing warning:** even a well-liked dense programme had ~11% say it felt overwhelming (one-week iCBT). Let people slow the path; don't fork it. [S] §1d

## 4. Psychoeducation core (original plain-English version, grade-8)

> When you're worried about how you come across, your attention turns inward. You start watching yourself — your voice, your face, your hands — and you build a picture of how you *think* you look, usually worse than the truth. To cope, you do little things to feel safer: rehearse every line, avoid eye contact, keep it short, or skip the situation entirely. Those moves feel helpful, but they stop you from ever finding out that the thing you fear mostly doesn't happen. Worrying beforehand and replaying it afterwards keep the picture alive. The way out is to turn your attention outward, drop one safety habit at a time, and check what really happened.

(Four sentences is enough for a first screen: inward attention → false self-picture → safety habits and avoidance block the test → the fix. The anticipatory/post-mortem sentence can be shown later, on the "after" screen.)

## (a) Minimal effective path — recommended sequence (one linear track)

| # | Step | Why (evidence) | What it replaces |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Why this happens** (the 4-sentence loop above + one tap: "which of these do you do?" picks the person's safety habits and self-focus cues) | Every programme opens with the model; picking your own habits makes step 3 concrete. (§2; Anxiety Canada "list the things you do to feel safer") | Self-check and the safety-habit comparison as separate tools |
| 2 | **Pick one brave step** from a short ladder (3–5 rungs, starting at 1–2/10) | Exposure is where the effect lives (Joyable d 1.4–1.8; Feske & Chambless). Early doing beats early thinking (de Jong & Lommen). | Graded ladder stays, but as the *picker* inside this step, not a separate tab |
| 3 | **Predict, then do it with attention out and the habit dropped** — "What do you fear will happen? How sure (0–100)?" + one line "I will look at ___ instead of myself" + the one habit to drop | Behavioural experiments ≥ restructuring-first and shorter (Johnco 2025); external focus + dropped SBs improves anxiety, beliefs and how others rate you (Wells & Papageorgiou; Taylor & Alden). | "Untangle a thought" as a standalone module |
| 4 | **Check what happened** — rate the same prediction, then (if recorded) listen back as a stranger *before* seeing your own rating | Expectancy violation predicts outcome (Johnco; Craske); video feedback works only after a specific prediction (Warnock-Parkes). | Observer listen-back stays, but only here, with prediction mandatory and no pre-filled sliders |
| 5 | **One line for next time** — "The thing I feared mostly didn't happen. Next rung: ___." Optional: "Stuck on a thought? Untangle it." | Maintenance/next-step is the last module everywhere; a short post-event note interrupts the "post-mortem." | Full thinking-trap worksheet becomes an optional branch here |

Default dose: one cycle of steps 2–5 per session, 10–15 minutes. Steps 1 runs once (re-openable). Progress = rungs climbed and "feared outcome didn't happen" count from *real* predictions only.

## (b) Copy examples (grade-8)

- **Step 1 title:** "Why speaking feels so hard" · body: the loop above · CTA: "Which of these do you do?" (checkboxes: "I rehearse every word", "I avoid eye contact", "I keep it short", "I watch my own voice/hands", "I skip it if I can").
- **Step 2:** "Pick one small thing to try this week. Start easy — you'll climb from here." Rung example: "Ask one question in a meeting."
- **Step 3:** "Before you go: what do you think will go wrong?" · "How sure are you? (0 = not at all, 100 = certain)" · "During it, put your attention on the people, not on yourself. Try it without [the habit you picked]."
- **Step 4:** "Done? Same question: what actually happened?" · "How much of your prediction came true? (0–100)" · If recording: "Listen as if a stranger made this. Then compare with what you expected."
- **Step 5:** "One line for next time. What did you learn? What's the next rung?" · Secondary link: "A thought keeps bugging you? Untangle it."
- **Never say** "cure", "treat", "therapy", "your anxiety disorder". Say "feels hard", "brave step", "what you feared", "what happened".

## (c) What to demote or remove from the current toolbox

- **Demote "Untangle a thought"** to an optional branch after step 4. Evidence: restructuring adds nothing measurable beyond exposure/experiments (Feske & Chambless; Hope 1995; de Jong & Lommen) and the experiment format does the same work faster (Johnco 2025). Keep the 8–9 traps list behind the link; don't show it in the main path. Remove the pre-filled 70→40 belief defaults (code-review B #2).
- **Fold the safety-habit comparison into steps 1 and 3** (pick the habit once, drop it during the step). A stand-alone comparison is a second place to do the same job; purely nudging habit-dropping without a brave step showed no effect (Zech 2025).
- **Fold the graded ladder into step 2** as the picker. Progression should be "prediction didn't come true" or "I did it with the habit dropped", not anxiety ≤ 4/10 (code-review B #4).
- **Move self-check out of the main path** (settings or first-run only) and keep it one question. It is screening, not a skill, and its current result copy over-implies diagnosis (code-review B #8).
- **Keep observer listen-back, but only inside step 4**, gated on a prior specific prediction, with sliders unset by default (Warnock-Parkes; code-review B #1).
- **Keep brave-step experiments** as the spine. Rename everything else so there is one noun: "brave step" (predict → do → check), not experiment / mission / real-world attempt.

## (d) Claims to avoid

- Don't claim the app "treats social anxiety" or is "as effective as therapy": the brief/single-session results are small, short-follow-up, partly non-clinical (§1d), and the Joyable effects are uncontrolled within-group d values.
- Don't say "thinking traps cause anxiety" or that challenging thoughts is required; outcome evidence says exposure alone ties (§1c).
- Don't say "stop your safety habits and your anxiety will drop": the digital habit-fading RCT was null; habit-dropping works inside a brave step with a prediction (§1a).
- Don't cite "90% of users improve" style figures (Joyable marketing, not peer-reviewed).
- Don't promise anxiety will fall to a target level; the goal is the prediction not coming true, not a SUDS drop (Craske 2014, prior research A4).
- Don't present video/listen-back as neutral evidence: without the prediction step it can backfire via processing biases (Warnock-Parkes 2017).
